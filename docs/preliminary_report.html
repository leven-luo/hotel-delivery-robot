2. Project description

A hotel delivery robot must carry a drink from the lobby to a guest room quickly without spilling or knocking it over. Our robot carries the payload on a tray that can tilt in pitch and roll: tilting the tray toward the effective gravity vector g − a reduces the load on the payload during braking and turning. In our initial simulator, the vehicle followed prescribed speed profiles while the controller adjusted tray pitch and roll, and liquid slosh was evaluated with an equivalent-pendulum model. In the next stage, we plan to control vehicle speed and tray tilt jointly and evaluate whether a tall cup (about 330 mL) remains upright during delivery. This is a new evaluation target; spill results from the first stage are not tip-over rates.

The problem combines optimal control (speed and tray commands jointly determine travel time and payload risk along the route) with reinforcement learning (acting on partial observations, e.g. deciding where to slow down without knowing whether someone is behind a door).

3. Tools

Existing code in the repository (Python/NumPy). A simplified simulator, not a fluid or full contact simulation: servo dynamics (pure delay, first-order lag, rate and angle limits), chassis tilt from acceleration and bumps, the equivalent-pendulum slosh model, and a route generator (acceleration, braking, turns, speed bumps, with a curriculum level from a fixed route to random routes). It is wrapped as a Gymnasium environment running at 100 Hz with a ±20° tray limit. The repository also contains the reference baselines, an evaluation script, a training entry point for SAC/PPO from Stable-Baselines3 (PyTorch), and unit tests with continuous integration. No training run has been completed yet.

Planned. Matplotlib for learning curves and result plots; simulator extensions for cup tipping and sliding, a speed action, and a simple corridor map with doors and stochastic pedestrians.

Hardware (planned, candidates only; nothing has been purchased, assembled or tested yet). A wheeled chassis with encoders and an IMU (candidate: Waveshare UGV02); a 3D-printed two-axis gimbal (fixed bracket, outer frame, food tray) driven by two hobby servos (candidate: DS3218) with bearings on the opposite side of each axis; an ESP32 tray controller; IMUs on the tray and inside the cup. The hardware control rate and tilt range will be set after system identification.

4. Algorithms

Reference baselines (implemented in v0). (i) Fixed tray. (ii) PID level-keeping: the tray IMU angle is driven to zero. (iii) Gravity feedforward: the tray is tilted to θ* = atan2(a, g + az) per axis from measured acceleration, compensating chassis tilt. (iv) Feedforward with 0.12 s preview: the acceleration is predicted by passing the prescribed speed commands through a motor-lag model. In v0, preview uses commands that are known in advance; in joint control and on hardware, every method will receive only information available at decision time (never future pedestrian events), and all compared methods will receive the same information.

Reinforcement learning (main method: SAC). Current task (v0, environment implemented, training pending): the observation contains IMU acceleration, chassis tilt, measured tray angle, the previous action, the last four accelerations, and optionally 0.2 s of prescribed acceleration commands and the distance to the next bump. The action is tray pitch and roll, either directly (±20°) or as a residual added to the gravity feedforward, which gives a physics-based starting point but no safety guarantee. The reward is 1 − (20 θ² + 0.05 θ̇² + 0.5 ‖Δu‖²) per step, where θ is the liquid tilt relative to the cup, with −100 and episode termination on a spill. Training uses an automatic curriculum (the route level is raised when the success rate exceeds 80%) and domain randomization of slosh frequency, damping and spill angle, servo lag, rate and delay, and chassis tilt. Planned extension: the action adds a target speed; the observation adds speed and observable map features (distance to the next door or intersection, visible free space); the reward becomes a time penalty per step, a bonus on arrival, large penalties on tip-over or collision, and a smoothness term. PPO is an optional comparison.

Extension if time permits: MPC. MPC would predict vehicle speed and tray motion over a 2 s horizon (40 steps of 0.05 s) and maximize progress minus a smoothness cost, subject to speed and tilt limits and a quasi-static no-tip constraint with margin. It would be compared with RL on the same routes and information. We claim no formal safety guarantee for any method; safety is evaluated through tip-over and collision rates.

Tip-over criterion (planned). As a simplified quasi-static criterion, the cup is at risk of tipping when the in-plane specific force exceeds r/h times the normal component (base radius over center-of-mass height). In reality a cup may slide first, or lift briefly and recover. On hardware, synchronized cup and tray IMUs will estimate the angle between the cup axis and the tray normal. The angle threshold and minimum duration will be calibrated on recordings of normal turns, bumps and actual tip-overs, and checked for false and missed detections. Detecting a tip-over is not the same as detecting a spill.

5. Planned work, experiments and expected results

  1. Experiment 1 (priority): does active tray control help? Same routes, speed profiles and physics; only the tray controller changes: fixed tray, gravity feedforward, feedforward with preview, and SAC or residual SAC once trained. Conditions: acceleration, braking, turning and speed bumps. Metrics: maximum liquid tilt, spill count and control effort in simulation. On hardware, if ready: tip-over counts for fixed vs. feedforward tray, with the sensor-based detection checked against labeled recordings.
  2. Experiment 2 (extension): can joint control shorten delivery? A 2 × 2 comparison of fixed or learned speed × fixed or active tray, on the same held-out routes and event seeds, with the same visible information, action limits and external protection rules. We will report the number of episodes, success rate, tip-over rate, collision rate and delivery time of successful episodes, interpreting time together with failure rates so that early failures do not look fast.

Hypotheses. We expect active tray control to reduce liquid slosh and tip-over during aggressive maneuvers. We will test whether joint control reduces delivery time at comparable tip-over and collision rates, and whether the learned policy slows down near blind doorways while keeping higher speeds in unobstructed corridors.
